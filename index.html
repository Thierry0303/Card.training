What happens when you tap your card?

You tap your card. The screen says "Approved" in under two seconds. But in those two seconds, your card information has crossed the globe and come back. Here's what actually happens.

It all starts with a question

The moment your card touches the reader, the payment terminal sends a message — a question — to your bank:

This person wants to spend £49.99 at Shop Name. Do you allow it?

Your bank has to answer that question, fast. If it says yes, the screen lights up green. If it says no, you get the embarrassing "Declined" message. This happens in about 1-3 seconds.

The message travels through four places

That question doesn't go directly from the shop to your bank. It travels through a relay system:

🛍️ Your tap at the till
→
🏦 Shop's bank
→
🌐 Visa/Mastercard network
→
💳 Your bank

Your bank checks your account, decides yes or no, and the answer travels back the exact same way. The whole conversation happens in seconds.

💡 Key insight: Visa and Mastercard don't hold your money
Visa and Mastercard are like telephone exchanges. They connect your bank to the shop's bank and make sure the message gets to the right place. They don't own any of your money — they just manage the network that payments flow through. Every bank around the world connects to them.

Why this matters

Before payment networks existed, your bank would need individual agreements with every other bank in the world. That's obviously impossible. Visa and Mastercard solved it by becoming the shared highway — every bank plugs into the network, and the network connects everyone together. It's like electrical outlets: you don't need different plugs for different houses because everyone uses the standard.

The four people in every payment

Every single card payment on Earth involves four different parties working together. Understanding who they are and what role they play explains almost everything about how payments work.

The four players

Role Who they are Example
Cardholder The person paying You, buying a coffee
Merchant The business being paid The coffee shop
Acquirer The merchant's bank Stripe, Square, Barclays
Issuer The cardholder's bank HSBC, Monzo, Chase

Plus there's a fifth player in the middle — the card scheme (Visa, Mastercard, American Express). They're the network that connects all four and sets the rules everyone must follow.

What each party does

You (the cardholder) tap your card and money moves from your account.

The merchant (shop) receives the customer's card information and requests approval. They want the money — that's why they accept cards in the first place.

The acquirer (merchant's bank) receives the payment request from the till, passes it to the card scheme, receives the decision, and then deposits money into the merchant's account days later after everything settles.

The issuer (your bank) receives the question "Does this person have enough money?" and answers yes or no based on your account balance and fraud checks.

🎯 The postal analogy
You want to send a letter (money) to someone in another country. You give it to your local post office (acquirer). They send it through the international postal network (Visa/Mastercard). It arrives at the recipient's local post office (issuer), which delivers it. Neither post office holds the letter — they just route it. The same is true with payments: money flows between banks, while Visa and Mastercard manage the network.

Why four parties?

Why not just connect you directly to the shop? Because banks need to protect themselves. They need independent confirmation from both sides. The issuer needs to make sure you have the money. The acquirer needs to make sure the transaction is legitimate. The card scheme acts as a neutral referee, making sure everyone plays by the rules.

Does the money move instantly?

Your card is approved instantly. But the actual movement of money is a separate process that happens hours or even days later. Here's why, and what it means.

Approval ≠ Payment

When your card is approved, your bank puts a hold on your money — like a restaurant putting a reservation under your name. The money is "reserved," but the restaurant hasn't charged you yet. The actual payment (called settlement) happens later as a separate process.

🏨 Hotels explain this perfectly
When you check in, the hotel "pre-authorizes" your card for, say, £200. You see it as "pending" in your app — the money isn't charged yet, just held. At checkout, they charge the exact amount you owe. The authorization (approval) and settlement (actual charge) are separate events.

What happens after approval

Here's the timeline:

  1. Moment of tap (instant): Your card is approved. You see "Approved" on the screen.
  2. End of business day (same day): The shop sends all its approved transactions to its bank in one big batch. This batch file is called "capturing" the transaction.
  3. Overnight (that night): The shop's bank sends these batch files to Visa/Mastercard, who calculate all the money moving between banks that day.
  4. Next 1-2 working days: Banks actually transfer money to each other. Your account is charged (the hold becomes a real charge) and the shop's account is credited.

So you can tap your card on a Friday afternoon, see it approved instantly, but the money doesn't actually move until Monday or Tuesday.

💡 Why do this instead of instant?
Processing every payment instantly would require banks to be connected 24/7, which would be expensive and slow. Instead, batching thousands of transactions together makes the system efficient. Visa processes trillions of pounds per year — batching transactions is the only way to do it without the whole system melting.

What this means for businesses

A busy café on Saturday might process £5,000 in payments, but that money doesn't appear in their bank account until Monday or Tuesday. For a small business relying on cash flow, this is a real problem. Some payment companies offer "instant settlement" — but they charge extra for it because they're lending the money in the meantime.

What those 16 numbers mean

Your card number isn't random. Every single digit has a specific purpose. Together, they tell the payment network everything it needs to know about you and your bank.

Breaking down a card number

4532 0151 1283 0366

Position What it means
1st digit: 4 This tells the world which card network it is. "4" = Visa. "5" = Mastercard. "3" = American Express or Diners Club. The network must be identifiable from just the first digit.
1st–6th digits (or 1st–8th) This is the bank identification number (BIN). It tells the network which bank issued the card. The payment system uses this to route the transaction to the right bank. Sort of like a postcode for banks.
7th–15th digits This is your unique account number — identifies you within your bank's system. No two cardholders at the same bank will have the exact same sequence here.
16th digit This is the check digit — it's calculated using a mathematical formula (Luhn algorithm) that detects typos. If you manually type your card number and make a mistake, this digit will be wrong and the payment network will reject it before even asking your bank.

Why this structure?

The first 6-8 digits (BIN) are the most important. When you tap or insert your card at a shop, the terminal reads these digits first and instantly knows which bank to contact. Without them, the network wouldn't know where to send the approval request.

The account number identifies you within that bank. The check digit is fraud prevention — if someone types a card number by mistake, or if there's a typo in transmission, this digit catches it.

🔐 Your card number tells a story
Someone with access to your card number can identify: (1) your bank, (2) what type of card it is, (3) roughly how old your card is, and (4) which country issued it — all from 16 digits. This is why keeping your card number secret is important, even if it's just for metadata.

Chip vs swipe vs tap — what's the difference?

You have three ways to use a card at a shop. They look similar, but they have completely different security properties. Understanding the difference explains why the world is moving away from swipe.

The magnetic stripe (swipe)

The black stripe on the back of your card is just magnetic tape — like a cassette tape. When you swipe, the reader reads it like a tape player. The information on the tape is:

  • Your card number
  • Your name
  • The expiry date
  • A 3-digit security code

The problem: it's the exact same data every single time. If a criminal puts a hidden skimmer on an ATM or café card reader, they capture this data once — and can use it forever to clone your card.

The chip (insert)

The gold or silver chip is a tiny computer. It's actually a secure processor with encryption built in. When you insert your card, here's what happens:

  1. The reader establishes a secure connection with the chip
  2. The chip performs calculations to generate a unique code for that specific transaction
  3. This code is sent to your bank for approval
  4. Even if someone captures this code, it only works for that one transaction at that one shop at that one time

This makes chip cloning essentially impossible. Every transaction is cryptographically different.

Contactless (tap)

Tapping your card uses radio waves (NFC — near field communication). It's the same chip technology as insertion, but wireless. Your card generates the same unique one-time code and sends it through the air instead of through a physical connection.

To prevent fraud, most cards have a £100 limit on contactless transactions. Above that, you need to enter your PIN to prove it's really you.

Swipe Chip Contactless
Speed Fast Slowest (3-5 seconds) Fastest (instant)
Security Low (static data) Very high (unique code) Very high (unique code)
Cloning risk High Nearly impossible Nearly impossible
Spend limit None None £100 (unless PIN used)
🌍 Why different countries do this differently
The USA was slow to adopt chip cards because they were inconvenient — they take 3-5 seconds versus instant swipe. Europe switched to chip cards years earlier because fraud was a bigger problem. Now the USA regrets it.

Why do cards get declined?

It's never the shop's fault when your card is declined — it's always your bank that said no. Here's why it happens and what you can do about it.

Your bank makes the decision

When a card is declined, the transaction flow stops at your bank. The shop's terminal sent the approval request to your bank, and your bank said "no." The shop never even sees why — they just get a decline code.

Your bank declines for specific, defensible reasons. They're not trying to annoy you — they're protecting themselves and you from fraud and overdrafts.

Common reasons for decline

What happened Why your bank said no What to do
Not enough funds Your balance is too low Check your balance or use a different card
Card reported lost You reported it lost or stolen Call your bank
Card expired The date on the front has passed Use a different card or renew
Bank suspects fraud This transaction looks unusual to the bank Check your app for alerts; call your bank
Daily limit reached You've already spent your limit today Wait until midnight or call your bank to raise limit
PIN wrong too many times Security lockout after failed attempts Contact your bank or use contactless instead
International transaction blocked Many banks block foreign transactions by default Tell your bank you're traveling or use a travel card

When it happens to you

Don't panic. Here's what to do:

  1. Check your banking app for any notifications or alerts
  2. If there's a fraud alert, verify it was you and approve
  3. Try using a different payment method if you have one
  4. Politely step aside if there's a queue behind you
  5. Call the number on the back of your card if unsure
🚨 Fraud prevention is aggressive
Banks decline transactions they're unsure about. It's frustrating when it's legitimate, but consider the alternative: if your card was stolen and the bank approved every transaction, you'd be in real trouble. Banks get paid for approvals but liable for fraud, so they lean toward caution.
